## How

The tldr is image → video turntable → splat.

A splat wants lots of photos of the same thing from known angles. You don't have that for someone who doesn't exist, so you get a video model to make it up. The catch is the video model makes up a slightly different person every frame, and the splat can only be as sharp as those frames agree with each other.

Basically everything below is me fighting that. The key unlock ended up being the [HumanOrbit](https://github.com/otiek/HumanOrbit) paper.

### 1. Image to video turntable, with a prompt

The obvious first go. Generate a front with [GPT Image 2.6](https://fal.ai/models/openai/gpt-image-2.6/flare/text-to-image), [edit](https://fal.ai/models/openai/gpt-image-2.6/flare/edit) it into a back view, then ask [Kling O3](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/kling-video/o3/standard/image-to-video) to orbit the camera around the person while they stand perfectly still. [VGGT](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vggt) guesses where the camera was for each frame, and [gsplat](https://github.com/nerfstudio-project/gsplat) trains on that.

The "stand perfectly still" bit is where it falls over. It was really hard to keep the person from moving. Ask for an orbit and half the time the camera stays put and the person turns on the spot instead, like they're on a lazy susan. When the camera does move, they shift their weight, sway their arms, or turn their head to follow you around. Video models really want people to *do* something.

That's fine for a video. It's fatal for a splat, which assumes the world holds still and only the camera moves. Every bit of fidgeting turns into blur.

### 2. Image to 3D to rendered turntable, recast with H3

If the cameras are guesses, why not render them myself?

So GPT Image makes a front and back, [Rodin](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/hyper3d/rodin) turns those into a rough 3D proxy, and [Blender](https://www.blender.org) renders an exact orbit around it. Every camera is known, and the proxy can't fidget. Then MiniMax's [H3 Recast](https://fal.ai/models/minimax/h3-max/recast) restyles the grey proxy video into a photoreal one, and the Blender cameras go straight into training. No guessing.

<Video src="/blog/splat-people/media/2-maya-proxy-recast.mp4" caption="Left: the Rodin proxy in Blender (head orbit, dolly out, body orbit, all one 14 second shot). Right: after H3 Recast." />

I went pretty deep on variations of this. A separate orbit just for the head. Painting eyes onto the proxy first, because otherwise Recast invented new ones every frame (and they followed the camera, which is unsettling). Squashing head and body into one 14 second shot so there's only one identity and one lighting. Starting the orbit at the back of the head so the loop seam lands on hair instead of a face.

It works on a neutral A-pose. Then I tried a pose.

<Video src="/blog/splat-people/media/2-nora-proxy-recast.mp4" caption="nora. The hand on the hip and the hair don't stay where the proxy puts them." />

<Splat src="/blog/splat-people/splats/nora" label="nora · recast" caption="Ghost arm, ghost leg." />

I threw a lot at the trainer after this. Longer training, more splats, optical flow to line the frames back up, a [SeedVR2](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/seedvr/upscale/video) upscaler pass. Most of it made things worse. Longer training just overfit, and the frames were only sliding about a pixel against the splat anyway.

The thing that eventually clicked: sharpness stalled at about a third of the source frames no matter what I did. The frames don't disagree on *where* things are, they disagree on *what* things are. Detail and shading change from frame to frame. The trainer was never the problem.

### 3. HumanOrbit (this was the key)

[HumanOrbit](https://github.com/otiek/HumanOrbit) is a [Wan 2.1](https://huggingface.co/Wan-AI/Wan2.1-I2V-14B-480P) LoRA trained to do exactly one thing: a full 360 orbit around a person who doesn't move. Which is precisely the thing I couldn't prompt my way into in step 1. No proxy, no Blender, no recast. One image in, 81 frames out.

<Video src="/blog/splat-people/media/3-humanorbit.mp4" caption="HumanOrbit for maya and nora, straight from the front image." />

The frames are tiny (544×736) and a bit soft. But they agree with each other, which turns out to matter way more.

  <Figure src="/blog/splat-people/media/3-ho-nora-eval.jpg" alt="nora splat render next to a held-out HumanOrbit frame" caption="nora, same deal." />
</Row>

| | route | PSNR ↑ | LPIPS ↓ | train |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nora | recast | 19.9 | 0.283 | 5.5 min |
| maya | recast | 23.4 | 0.218 | 5.8 min |
| nora | HumanOrbit | **26.0** | **0.071** | 2.6 min |
| maya | HumanOrbit | **26.5** | **0.076** | 2.6 min |

(Scored against held-out frames. The HumanOrbit frames are lower res than the recast ones so it's not perfectly apples to apples, but nora going from ghost limbs to an actual person is hard to argue with.)

Funnily enough, upscaling the orbit 2× with [SeedVR2](https://github.com/numz/ComfyUI-SeedVR2_VideoUpscaler) before training scored *worse*. The upscaler invents fresh detail per frame, which is the exact problem again.

### 4. Anchoring the face

HumanOrbit's weak spot is resolution. The original image is 1088×1920 and the face is lovely. By the time it's been through a 480p orbit it's... fine. Soft.

(Also, a funny one. HumanOrbit saves its frames as quality 75 JPEGs. I patched it to write PNGs and that alone bumped the scores. Free win.)

So - why not train on the original image too? It's basically frame 0 of the orbit, so I know exactly where its camera is. After the normal training, a second pass mixes in the full res front image.

The first attempt went weird. With only one view to match, the trainer happily cheats and pushes splats forwards and backwards however it likes. Eyes sank into the head, and the embroidery floated off the dress. The fix was locking depth to what the orbit had already learnt, and keeping splats thin along the view ray. The front image can repaint the surface, but it can't reshape it.

<Figure src="/blog/splat-people/media/4-anchor-front.jpg" alt="Four splat renders of nora's face from the front, getting sharper left to right" caption="Straight on. Left to right: 480p, 480p + anchor, 720p, 720p + anchor." />

Straight on, it's a big jump. Eyes, lashes, skin and earrings all come back.

The catch is turning away. The anchor only knows what the face looks like from the front, so at 480p it paints a sharp face over a soft head, and 40° off axis they stop lining up. Double eyes. Slightly horror movie.

<Figure src="/blog/splat-people/media/4-anchor-40.jpg" alt="The same four renders from 40 degrees to the side; the 480p anchored one has doubled eyes" caption="Same four, 40° off axis. The 480p anchor smears. At 720p it holds." />

Bumping the orbit itself to 720p fixed that. It was weirdly the biggest single quality jump of the whole project, and it still only takes about 2.5 minutes to generate. With a better head underneath, the anchor has something to agree with.

<Row>
  <Splat src="/blog/splat-people/splats/ho2-nora-hi" label="nora · 720p" caption="720p orbit, no anchor." />
  <Splat src="/blog/splat-people/splats/ho2-nora-hi-a" label="nora · 720p + anchor" caption="Plus the front image. Hit face." />

The numbers are a bit funny here. Held-out PSNR barely moves (24.0 → 24.1), which makes sense - it's being scored against soft orbit frames, not the sharp original. Sharpness goes from 0.40 to 0.54 though, and you can see it.

It's not free. The anchored one is 1.35M splats against 640k, so it's twice the download and takes a little longer to load.
