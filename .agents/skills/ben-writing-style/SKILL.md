# Ben's writing style

Write like a technically literate founder thinking in public: conversational, candid, curious, slightly self-deprecating, and comfortable leaving some uncertainty unresolved.

## Voice

- Write in the first person and ground claims in lived experience.
- Sound like a person talking through an idea, not an expert delivering a verdict.
- Be honest about uncertainty. Use phrases such as “I think,” “perhaps,” “it feels like,” and “I go back and forth” where they are genuine.
- State personal conclusions plainly, especially when they are uncomfortable: “I regret pivoting.” “Uncomfortable is the word I'd use.”
- Add dry humour or a parenthetical aside occasionally. Keep it understated rather than joke-heavy.
- Use casual language sparingly: “kinda,” “weirdly,” “guilty,” “jamming on,” “fancy, shiny,” or “have at it.” Do not force slang.
- Prefer Australian/British spelling where natural, such as “monetise” and “learnt.”

## Shape the piece

1. Focus on one idea, tension, or analogy.
2. Open directly with a claim, confession, or small observation. Skip scene-setting and throat-clearing.
3. Move from a personal example to the broader idea.
4. Complicate the argument rather than pretending it is certain. Contrast the obvious narrative with what Ben has actually noticed.
5. End with a plain conclusion, open question, or honest admission. Do not manufacture an inspirational takeaway.

Use memorable, conversational titles. A slightly odd phrase or familiar technical analogy works well: “Change Constipation,” “The new .vimrc,” or “Tabs as nodes.”

## Rhythm and mechanics

- Keep paragraphs short, usually one to three sentences.
- Mix straightforward sentences with fragments for emphasis.
- Use contractions throughout.
- Use rhetorical questions to frame genuine uncertainty, not as a repeated “Problem? Solution.” device. Avoid chains of punchy mini-examples.
- Use parentheses and dashes for asides, pivots, and qualifications.
- Use occasional one-line paragraphs to let an admission or question land.
- Prefer concrete examples, products, conversations, and numbers over abstract claims.
- Explain technical ideas through familiar comparisons instead of piling on jargon.
- Keep most posts concise. Stop once the central thought has landed.
- Preserve a few natural rough edges, but do not deliberately add typos or broken grammar.

## Avoid

- Generic AI prose such as “In today's rapidly evolving landscape.”
- Corporate polish, marketing copy, or heroic founder language.
- Long introductions, formal transitions, and exhaustive explanations.
- Overconfident predictions or claims unsupported by personal experience.
- Dense lists unless the argument genuinely benefits from contrasting points.
- Flowery imagery, forced profundity, or a perfectly tied-up conclusion.
- Excessive em dashes, punchy fragments, rhetorical questions, or slang. These are accents, not the whole voice.

## Site post conventions

For a post in `src/content/blog/`, use this frontmatter:

```yaml
---
title: Post title
date: "YYYY-MM-DD"
excerpt: A direct one-sentence summary in the same voice.
visible: true
---
```

Repeat the title as an H1, write the body in MDX, and sign off with:

```markdown
\- Ben
```

## Final pass

Before finishing, check that the draft:

- sounds spoken rather than presented;
- contains a concrete personal anchor;
- makes one clear observation without overstating it;
- includes uncertainty only where Ben would genuinely feel it;
- has no generic filler or unnecessary summary;
- is a little less polished, shorter, and more human than the default draft.
